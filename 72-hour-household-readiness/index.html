72-Hour Household Readiness: A Practical Plan That Uses What You Already Have

Preparedness works better when it becomes part of normal life. Instead of buying a pile of emergency products, build a small buffer around the things your household already depends on.

Ready.gov guidance includes at least three days of water and nonperishable food in a basic emergency supply kit, along with lighting, communication, first aid and other essentials.

Water

Use an official local recommendation as your baseline. U.S. Ready guidance commonly uses one gallon per person per day for at least three days. Store water where it is accessible, rotate it appropriately, and account for household-specific needs.

Food

Choose foods your household already eats. Keep a short buffer of familiar shelf-stable or easy-to-prepare food, then rotate it through normal meals. This reduces forgotten stock and makes replenishment easier.

Light and phone power

Test flashlights before you need them. Keep a charged power bank and a spare cable in a known location. If one device is the only way you communicate, pay, navigate and access contacts, it is a major single point of failure.

Cash and payment backups

A small amount of cash can be useful when electronic payment systems are unavailable. Keep at least two usable payment methods rather than assuming one card, wallet or device will always work.

Communication

Write down critical contacts. If your phone is lost, damaged or out of power, you should still be able to reach the people and services that matter most.

Medication and hygiene

Household readiness must fit the people in the home. Consider prescriptions, mobility needs, allergies, infants, pets and other requirements. Generic checklists are a starting point, not a substitute for personal planning.

The monthly reset

Once a month, spend 15 minutes checking food, water, lighting, batteries, backup power, cash, important contacts and digital backups. The goal is a system that stays usable without requiring constant attention.
