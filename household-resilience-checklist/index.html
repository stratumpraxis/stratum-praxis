Household Resilience Checklist: 30 Things to Check Before Life Gets Disrupted

A resilient household is not a home full of emergency gear. It is a home where ordinary life can keep moving when one thing fails: a power outage, a missed paycheck, a broken phone, a spoiled grocery run, a lost wallet, or a week when your energy drops.

The fastest way to improve resilience is to find the weakest system first.

1. Food

Keep a small buffer of foods you already eat, create an “eat first” area in the fridge, replenish staples after using them, and avoid buying more than you can realistically store and rotate. Food resilience is less about stockpiling and more about preventing the cycle of overbuying, forgetting and replacing.

2. Water and power

Have drinking water, a working flashlight, a charged power bank, a spare charging cable and a small amount of cash. U.S. Ready guidance recommends at least three days of water and food in a basic emergency supply kit, including roughly one gallon of water per person per day.

3. Money

Know your fixed monthly costs. Identify subscriptions you no longer use, recurring fees you tolerate out of habit, and repeated purchases caused by losing or forgetting things. Small monthly leaks become meaningful annual losses.

4. Home systems

Give keys, wallet, chargers and important documents fixed homes. The goal is not aesthetic organization. The goal is reducing repeated search time and avoiding failures caused by one missing item.

5. Low-cost leisure

Resilience also includes recovery. Keep several ways to rest, socialize or reset your mood that do not require shopping every time. A household that can recover without constant spending is easier to maintain.

6. Backups

Back up important files, keep more than one usable payment method, store critical contacts offline and create at least one alternative route for essential supplies. A single point of failure is often more important than a low checklist score.

What should you fix first?

Prioritize weaknesses in this order: things that can stop daily life, failures that become expensive, recurring time friction, then quality-of-life gaps. The lowest score is useful, but impact matters more.
