How much is your AI & SaaS stack really costing?

The monthly price is usually the easiest number to see—and often the least useful one. Before renewing, adding seats or buying another AI tool, convert the stack into an annual decision you can actually compare.

The quick check
  • Annualize every recurring subscription and AI add-on.
  • Separate active use from “we might need it later.”
  • Flag duplicate tools that solve the same workflow.
  • Include maintenance, human review and switching cost—not just license price.
  • Use a stop/hold condition before committing more budget.

1. Start with annualized spend

A $49 tool feels small in a monthly budget. Across a year, five $49 tools are $2,940 before tax, implementation time, additional seats or usage-based charges. The first useful move is simply to make the full-year number visible.

Annualized license cost = monthly recurring cost × 12 + annual plans + usage-based charges you reasonably expect.

This is not a savings estimate. It is a decision baseline. If the baseline is wrong, every ROI claim built on top of it is also wrong.

2. Compare cost with actual use

Do not treat “the account exists” as evidence that the tool is valuable. Look for active users, workflows completed, outputs used, time avoided, errors reduced or revenue supported. If none of those are observable, classify the value as unproven rather than assuming it is zero or positive.

Whether overlap exists.
What does keeping it avoid?
Time, delay, error, risk or lost revenue.

3. Look for overlap before cancellation

Duplicate software is not always waste. Two tools may serve different teams, permissions or failure modes. But overlap should be explicit. If two tools generate meeting summaries, automate the same workflow or provide similar AI chat capability, write down why both still need to exist.

4. Add the hidden cost of replacing a tool

A cheap internal replacement can become expensive if it needs security work, monitoring, vendor API maintenance, break/fix support or a person who owns it. Build-versus-buy decisions should include the cost of keeping the replacement working after launch.

5. Decide with a threshold, not a feeling

A useful renewal review ends with one of five actions: keep, reduce, consolidate, review further or cancel. Define what evidence would change the decision. That keeps the process reversible and stops “we already paid for it” from becoming the reason to renew again.

What not to do

Do not promise a savings percentage before you have usage and workflow evidence. Do not cancel operational software just because another tool looks cheaper. Do not count hypothetical productivity gains as realized ROI. The goal is not “cut SaaS”; it is to make each renewal legible enough to keep, change or stop for a reason.
